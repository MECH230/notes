23  Lagrange’s Equations

23.1 D’Alembert’s Principle

Newton’s second law for particle \(i\) is \({\bf F}_i^{\text{tot}} = m_i{\bf a}_i\), where \({\bf F}_i^{\text{tot}}\) includes both applied forces and constraint forces. Rearranging as \({\bf F}_i^{\text{tot}}-m_i{\bf a}_i={\bf 0}\) and dotting with a virtual displacement \(\delta{\bf r}_i\), then summing over all particles: \[\begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^N\lp{\bf F}_i^{\text{tot}}-m_i{\bf a}_i\rp\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i = 0. \end{align}\] Splitting \({\bf F}_i^{\text{tot}}\) into applied forces \({\bf F}_i\) and constraint forces \({\bf f}_i^c\), and restricting attention to workless constraints (rigid smooth guides, inextensible cables, ideal pins) for which \(\sum_i{\bf f}_i^c\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i=0\) for every admissible \(\delta{\bf r}_i\), we obtain D’Alembert’s principle: \[\begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^N\lp{\bf F}_i-m_i{\bf a}_i\rp\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i = 0 \qquad \text{for all admissible }\delta{\bf r}_i. \end{align}\] This is a virtual-work statement for dynamics: the applied forces, together with the fictitious “inertia force” \(-m_i{\bf a}_i\), do zero virtual work. Unlike the static case, this holds identically (it is just Newton’s law), not as a special condition.

23.2 From D’Alembert’s Principle to Lagrange’s Equations

Substitute \(\delta{\bf r}_i = \sum_j(\partial{\bf r}_i/\partial q_j)\delta q_j\) and use the independence of the \(\delta q_j\). The applied-force term is exactly the virtual work from the previous lecture: \[\begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^N{\bf F}_i\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i = \sum_{j=1}^n Q_j\,\delta q_j. \end{align}\] The inertia term requires more work: \[\begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^N m_i{\bf a}_i\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i = \sum_{j=1}^n\lp\sum_{i=1}^N m_i{\bf a}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j}\rp\delta q_j. \end{align}\] Focus on the bracketed coefficient for a fixed \(j\). Using the product rule, \[\begin{align} m_i{\bf a}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{d}{dt}\lp m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j}\rp - m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j}\rp. \end{align}\] Two identities, both proved by direct differentiation of \({\bf r}_i(q_1,\dots,q_n,t)\), close the derivation: \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{\partial{\bf v}_i}{\partial \dot q_j} \qquad\text{(cancellation of the dots, Lecture 38)},\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j}\rp = \frac{\partial {\bf v}_i}{\partial q_j}. \end{align}\] Substituting both identities: \[\begin{align} m_i{\bf a}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{d}{dt}\lp m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf v}_i}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf v}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot q_j}\lp\tfrac12 m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot{\bf v}_i\rp\rp-\frac{\partial}{\partial q_j}\lp\tfrac12 m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot{\bf v}_i\rp. \end{align}\] Summing over all particles and recognizing the total kinetic energy \(T=\sum_i\tfrac12 m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot{\bf v}_i\): \[\begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^N m_i{\bf a}_i\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}. \end{align}\]

ImportantLagrange’s Equations

D’Alembert’s principle, \(\sum_j\leb Q_j-\lp\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}\rp\reb\delta q_j=0\), holds for arbitrary independent \(\delta q_j\), so each bracket must vanish: \[\begin{align} \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} = Q_j, \qquad j=1,\dots,n. \end{align}\] This is a set of \(n\) second-order ODEs — exactly enough to determine the motion of an \(n\)-DOF system, and workless constraint forces never appear.

23.3 Conservative Forces and the Lagrangian

If every applied force is conservative, \(Q_j\) derives from a potential energy \(U(q_1,\dots,q_n)\) (possibly \(t\)-dependent) via \[\begin{align} Q_j = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial q_j}. \end{align}\] Since \(U\) does not depend on \(\dot q_j\), \(\partial U/\partial\dot q_j = 0\), so we may move it inside the time-derivative term for free. Defining the Lagrangian \[\begin{align} L(q,\dot q,t) \equiv T-U, \end{align}\] Lagrange’s equations for a fully conservative system take their most compact form: \[\begin{align} \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = 0, \qquad j=1,\dots,n.} \end{align}\] If some forces are conservative (folded into \(U\), hence into \(L\)) and others are not (springs with damping, applied external moments, friction), the general form is retained for the nonconservative part: \[\begin{align} \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = Q_j^{\text{nc}}, \qquad j=1,\dots,n. \end{align}\]

23.4 Procedure

NoteRecipe
  1. Choose independent generalized coordinates \(q_1,\dots,q_n\) equal in number to the DOF.
  2. Write position vectors \({\bf r}_i(q,t)\), differentiate to get \({\bf v}_i\), and form \(T=\sum_i\tfrac12 m_i{\bf v}_i\cdot{\bf v}_i\) (for rigid bodies, use the Koenig decomposition from the rigid-body chapter).
  3. Identify the potential energy \(U\) of all conservative forces and any remaining generalized forces \(Q_j^{\text{nc}}\) from nonconservative forces (via virtual work, Lecture 38).
  4. Form \(L=T-U\) and compute \(\partial L/\partial\dot q_j\), then \(\frac{d}{dt}(\cdot)\), then subtract \(\partial L/\partial q_j\).
  5. Set the result equal to \(Q_j^{\text{nc}}\) (zero if the system is fully conservative) for each \(j\) to get the equations of motion.

23.5 Worked Example: Simple Pendulum, Revisited

Return to the pendulum of the previous lecture, \(q_1=\theta\). The velocity of the mass is \({\bf v}=\ell\dot\theta\,{\bf e}_\theta\), so \[\begin{align} T = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2, \qquad U = -mg\ell\cos\theta \quad(\text{measuring from the pivot}). \end{align}\] The Lagrangian is \(L=T-U=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\cos\theta\). Then \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} = m\ell^2\dot\theta, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}\rp = m\ell^2\ddot\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = -mg\ell\sin\theta. \end{align}\] Lagrange’s equation gives \[\begin{align} m\ell^2\ddot\theta+mg\ell\sin\theta = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta+\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta=0, \end{align}\] identical to the moment equation about the pivot obtained by Newton–Euler methods — but here it fell out of two scalar energy functions, with no free-body diagram and no pin reaction ever computed.

23.6 Why This Works: A Comparison

Newton–Euler Lagrangian
Unknowns tracked Positions, all forces (including constraints) Generalized coordinates only
Free-body diagrams Required for every body Not required
Constraint forces Must be solved for for or eliminated by hand Never appear
Vector/scalar Vector equations Scalar equations (\(T\), \(U\))
Best suited to Few bodies, forces of direct interest Many DOF, complex constraints

Both methods produce the same equations of motion — they must, since Lagrange’s equations were derived from Newton’s second law. The Lagrangian approach simply automates the elimination of workless constraint forces that Newton–Euler methods require you to introduce and then cancel by hand.

23.7 Summary

D’Alembert’s principle, \(\sum_i({\bf F}_i-m_i{\bf a}_i)\cdot\delta{\bf r}_i=0\), converts dynamics into a virtual-work statement. Expanding in generalized coordinates yields Lagrange’s equations: \[\begin{align} \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} = Q_j, \qquad j=1,\dots,n. \end{align}\] For conservative forces, \(Q_j=-\partial U/\partial q_j\), and with \(L=T-U\): \[\begin{align} \frac{d}{dt}\lp\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}\rp-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = 0. \end{align}\] Nonconservative generalized forces \(Q_j^{\text{nc}}\) appear on the right-hand side when present.
